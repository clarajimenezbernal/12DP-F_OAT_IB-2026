**Q12: Are there any highly conserved regions across the three species in the CDS sequences?**  
The highest regions that are conserved across the three species only reach 5 corresponding nucleotides. That is due to the fact that the three species are extremely genetically different.

**Q13: Are there any sequence positions with variants across the three species, either substitutions or gaps in the CDS sequences?**
Yes, there are 8 nucleotides that have mutated to the same one between the two other homolog species but it is only one nucleotide at a time. 

**Q14: Are there any highly conserved regions across the three species in the protein sequences?**
The longest conserved regions across the three protein sequences are formed by 7 amino-acids. This might not be considered a high conservation but, again, the three species are too different among them to have any longer coincidental regions. 

**Q15: Are there any sequence positions with variants across the three species, either substitutions or gaps in the protein sequences?**
There are only four amino-acids the mutation of which is the same in both Toxoplasma gondii and Rattus norvegicus.

**Q17: Are all nucleotide sequence variants accompanied by a corresponding, equivalent amino-acid sequence variant?**
No, not all nucleotide variants correspond to an amino-acid sequence variant because one amino-acid can correspond to more than one codon. Therefore, different combinations of nucleotides can lead to the same amino-acid combinations.

**Q18: Do the three species appear more similar at the CDS or protein level? Why?**
The three species appear more similar at the CDS level because of what has already been explained above. The change in one nucleotide does not necessarily mean the change in the amino-acid. Amino-acids are defined by codons, which are groups of three nucleotides. However, not all combinations of three nucleotides have their own corresponding amino-acid. Each amino-acid is usually paired with between 2 and 4 different codons.
