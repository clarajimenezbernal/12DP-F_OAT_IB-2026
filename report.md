## **Background**
### _Protein and disease_
Human Ornithine Aminotransferase (hOAT), otherwise known as ornithine δ-aminotransferase (δ-OAT), is an enzyme found mostly concentrated in the liver, intestines and kidneys. Its main biological function is to maintain the ornithine level in mitochondria stable. OAT is an amino acid found in the liver that converts a toxic chemical, nitrogen metabolite ammonia, into urea, an organic waste. Ornithine Aminotransferase Deficiency can result in hyperammonemia in the neonatal stages of life, which leads to seizures and mental disorders. On the other hand, in older children, its deficiency causes a rare recessive inherited disease that causes progressive vision loss called Gyrate Atrophy (Sun 2017, Floriani 2026).

Gyrate atrophy of the choroid and retina (GACR) is a rare disease inherited in an autosomal recessive manner. Although the OAT protein functions in mitochondria, the OAT gene is located on an autosome rather than in mitochondrial DNA. Therefore, the disease does not follow maternal mitochondrial inheritance.It can be predominantly found in finland, with a prevalence estimated to be around 1 in 50 000 (Ayman G. Elnahry,2023).

According to Palmer et al. (2023), the median age at diagnosis among the 18 patients included in their study was eight years. Common initial manifestations include myopia and night vision problems. As the disease progresses, patients may develop a gradual loss of peripheral visual field, which can eventually lead to severe visual impairment.

Currently, there is no definitive cure for GACR. However, dietary interventions aimed at reducing plasma ornithine levels may help manage the condition and potentially slow disease progression.

Although gyrate atrophy is associated with OAT deficiency and elevated ornithine levels, the mechanisms through which ornithine accumulation contributes to retinal degeneration are not fully understood. Different hypotheses have been proposed to explain this process, highlighting the complexity of the relationship between altered amino acid metabolism and retinal damage.

### _Genomics_
The understanding of genomics is of significant importance in order to study OAT deficiency and fully comprehend it. Humans have 23 pairs of chromosomes, which consist of the individual’s entire genome. Most of the genome can be found in almost every cell. Genes are specific regions of DNA located within the chromosome. The fixed location where it is found is known as the locus. What causes each cell to be different and, therefore, have different functions, is what is called gene expression. This process happens when the DNA transcription turns it into RNA. 

DNA transcription starts with an RNA polymerase that binds onto the promoter of a gene and copies the information onto itself. When the transcription is complete, there will be a chain made up of exons and introns, which are the segments that contain information for the protein coding and the segments that do not, respectively. 

However, this RNA will go through a process called splicing, in which the introns are discarded and a chain of mRNA is produced. This mRNA is the sequence that proteins translate from. Nevertheless, there is also the possibility that the RNA goes through a similar process named alternative splicing. This allows humans to create a wide variety of isoforms from the same gene. Moreover, a single gene does not only produce just one mRNA, but many. This is due to the fact that mRNA is very fragile, and its survival rate is low. The ones that do survive get selected by the ribosomes, which get translated into proteins. 
This process is called translation, and happens inside the ribosomes. Codons are what mRNA is composed of, which are sets of three nucleotides. Transfer RNAs are molecules that are bound to a specific amino acid and select the unique codon it codes from. 

Every gene has a reference sequence that is used to identify and compare variant mutations. Mutations can affect only one nucleotide (point mutations) as well as a large sequence of nucleotides. Point mutations can be silent, missense or nonsense. Silent mutations do not change the amino acid, while the missense mutations do. These mutations can either be similar in terms of characteristics which make them conservative mutations, or be completely different (non-conservative). On the other hand, the nonsense mutations happen when the codon that was going to code into an amino acid becomes a stop codon, which immediately puts a stop to the protein synthesis. 

###  _Homologs_
In order to obtain a wider vision of what the mutation and corresponding disease entail, there has been research throughout the project involving non-human species also affected by said mutation. In this case, the two organisms chosen were _Toxoplasma gondii_ and _Rattus norvegicus_. The sequence from the two was extracted media Blast in a FASTA file from both their CDS in the form of a nucleotide chain and their protein, which showcasts amino acids. 

The first species, _Toxoplasma gondii_, was given by the organisation of the project. The organism is a protozoan parasite of only 13 chromosomes, responsible for the very famous disease toxoplasmosis, which, according to the National Institute of Health, around 35% of the population carries. (Bisetegn H, Debash H, Ebrahim H, 2023) This parasite is only able to carry out sexual reproduction while being inside the intestine of a feline. However, it can still infect all mammals. 

It is quite relevant for it to be studied during this assignment because the organism presents its own Ornithine Aminotransferase enzyme and, moreover, scientists have been able to carry out a significantly extenseive research on the parasite due to the fact that it is accessible for them to cultivate it in a petri dish inside a lab. 

The other species, _Rattus norvegicus_, was chosen because of its affinity with the human OAT sequence, both for the CDS and the protein (Ana Belén Rojas and L. Javier Palomo, 2007)
These animals live in urban areas all around the world and reproduce sexually throughout the months between March and October. 



Because _Toxoplasma gondii_ is evolutionarily distant from mammals, its nucleotide and protein sequences showed too many differences, making it difficult to identify regions conserved across species. Therefore, _Pongo abelii_ (the Sumatran orangutan) was included as an additional species because of its closer evolutionary relationship to humans. This comparison allows for a clearer identification of conserved regions in both nucleotide and protein sequences.

### _Structure_

In order to analyse the protein’s structure, the sequence ID was retrieved from the BLAST website and mapped in the PDB. In this manner, it was determined that the experimental data snapshot was obtained through X-Ray Diffraction, exhibiting an image resolution of 1.93Å.


This study aims to investigate the molecular basis of gyrate atrophy associated with the OAT gene, focusing on the Arg180Thr variant. Through bioinformatics analyses, the study examines the genetic and protein sequence differences between the reference and disease-associated states, explores the potential structural and functional consequences of the variant, and compares OAT across humans and other selected species to identify conserved features and assess their possible biological significance. Finally, these analyses aim to explain how changes in the OAT gene and its encoded protein may contribute to the development of the disease and affect normal biological function.


## **Results**
  ### _Genomics_
The BLAST results that came from inserting the protein sequences into said website showed a handful of information. First of all, the mutant protein and the wild type amino-acid are not the same type. While the wild type is Arginine, the mutant type is Threonine. Therefore, the mutation is considered to be a substitution because one nucleotide is interchanged by a different one during transcription. 

Between Arginine and Threonine there are some differing properties that are worth mentioning. Whereas Arginine is a large molecule with a 174.20 g/mol molecular weight, Threonine is considered to be small to medium and its molecular weight is 119.12 g/mol. The reason for this is that the first molecule features a long 3-carbon aliphatic chain ending in a complex guanidino group and the second one only a short hydroxyethyl group.

Also, Arginine is highly polar and amphipathic whereas Threonine is only polar. Arginine’s guanidino group is very hydrophilic but its carbon backbone offers a slight hydrophobic character closer to the main chain. Threonine is polar because its side chain contains a hydrophilic hydroxyl group (-OH) which is capable of forming hydrogen bonds. At a neutral pH, Arginine has a positive charge and side chain, which is very basic, stays protonated under physiological conditions. Threonine, on the other hand, does not have a charge and its side chain is neither acidic nor basic. 
Threonine and Arginine have different conformational freedom. Threonine has a very small amount of freedom due to the fact that it has a ramification formed by a hydroxyl group and a methyl group and that causes it to collide with the main structure of the protein when it is trying to rotate. Biologically, that means it is a very rigid molecule and is able to maintain the -OH group facing a specific direction which is great for phosphorylation. Arginine, however, is the opposite. It has a very high conformational freedom because it has a linear chain which means that all four angles of the molecule are free. This allows the molecule to bend and move in order to latch onto other molecules such as DNA. Nonetheless, this also means that the bonds must be really strong (like multiple hydrogen bonds) in order for the molecule to stay still. 

This substitution can also change the interactions that happen between these molecules and other ones. Because Arginine is polar, it is able to form salt bridges. However, Threonine is neutral (or apolar) and, therefore, there is a loss of salt bridges. 

In the molecule, the overall structure and oligomeric state remain the same, which means that the mutation does not carry extremely significant structural changes. However, the change can cause small rearranges such as Gly320’ shifting towards the active site. This can lead to steric interferences with the carboxyl group and make it difficult for the other substrates to bind onto the molecule

Also, this ⍺-carboxylate ionic interaction anchors the substrate in the one correct orientation. When Arginine is lost in the mutation, the binding affinity of the carboxyl group for ornithine is also lost. A different Arginine from a different position might intervene and take over.

Therefore, this mutation means two very significant things for the protein. The first one is that it increases its structural stability. The second one is that it disrupts what should be the actual positioning of the substrate in order for the molecule to undergo normal catalysis. Thus, the protein’s ability to recognise a substrate and its reaction specificity are more altered than the actual overall structure.

###  _Homologs_
When working with the sequence alignments of the three original species, which were Homo sapiens, Toxoplasma gondii and Rattus norvegicus, the last two being the homologs, it became obvious that the differences among their protein sequences were abysmal. The longest conserved regions across the three protein sequences are formed by 7 amino-acids. This might not be considered a high conservation but, the three species are too different among them to have any longer coincidental regions. There are only four amino-acids the mutation of which is the same in both Toxoplasma gondii and Rattus norvegicus. 

Also, there are very few sequence positions with variants across the three species. Only four amino-acids the mutation of which is the same in both Toxoplasma gondii and Rattus norvegicus. Furthermore, some regions are more conserved than others. For instance, both the Query range 4: 181 to 240  and the Query range 5: 241 to 300 have 39 amino-acids conserved. However, the Query range 1: 1 to 60 and the Query range 8: 421 to 439 have 7 and 5 amino-acids conserved respectively.

Not all nucleotide variants correspond to an amino-acid sequence variant because one amino-acid can correspond to more than one codon. Therefore, different combinations of nucleotides can lead to the same amino-acid combinations. The three species appear more similar at the CDS level because of what has already been explained above. The change in one nucleotide does not necessarily mean the change in the amino-acid. Amino-acids are defined by codons, which are groups of three nucleotides. However, not all combinations of three nucleotides have their own corresponding amino-acid. Each amino-acid is usually paired with between 2 and 4 different codons.

The multiple sequence alignment results showed that the addition of _Pongo abelii_ to the comparison with human OAT and _Rattus norvegicus_ facilitated the identification of highly conserved regions in both nucleotide and protein sequences.

In the nucleotide sequence, there were several instances where the human and _P. abelii_ sequences coincided with over 60 nucleotides (for example, human positions 1281 to 1440), which made the conserved regions easier to distinguish. In total, three highly conserved regions were found between the human nucleotide sequence and those of _P. abelii_ and _Rattus norvegicus_:

- Human positions 901 to 960 (query 16), with only one difference in _P. abelii_ and four in _R. norvegicus_.
  
- Positions 361 to 420 (query 7), completely identical to _P. abelii_ and with only five differences from _R. norvegicus_.
  
- Positions 121 to 180 (query 3), with one difference from the Sumatran orangutan and six from the rat.

Furthermore, in several positions, a substitution occurred in both species at the same time, and in most of these cases the substitution was in the same nucleotide (for instance, at position 606, where a guanine is changed to an adenine in both the orangutan and the rat).

The analysis of the protein sequences also revealed similarities between the human and orangutan sequences, and again three highly conserved regions were identified:

- Aminoacids positions 102 to 166 (queries 2 and 3), where the orangutan is identical and the rat has only two variants.
  
- Positions 241 to 300 (query 5), with one difference in _P. abelii_ and two in _R. norvegicus_.
  
- Positions 181 to 240 (query 4), where only the rat shows four differences.
  
Additionally, there were four positions where both species had variants (the last three also shared with _T. gondii_): 2, 180, 353 and 389.

## **Methods**

Scientific literature was consulted through databases such as PubMed and MeSH to gather information about OAT, OAT deficiency and its associated disease, as well as the functional roles of specific amino acids, including Arg180.

BLAST was used to verify that the provided wild-type and mutant nucleotide and protein sequences corresponded to human OAT. The results were evaluated using parameters such as accession type, query coverage and E-value. The information associated with the BLAST results was also examined to identify the gene and determine its chromosomal location and locus, using additional databases such as OMIM.

The wild-type nucleotide and protein sequences were then used to identify homologous sequences from other species. BLAST searches were performed by specifying the organism of interest, allowing the identification of OAT homologues in _Rattus norvegicus_, _Pongo abelii_ and _Toxoplasma gondii_.

Sequence alignments were performed to compare the wild-type and mutant human sequences and to examine differences associated with the mutation. Multiple sequence alignments were also used to compare the human wild-type sequences with their homologues from the other species, identifying similarities and conserved regions at both the nucleotide and protein levels.
